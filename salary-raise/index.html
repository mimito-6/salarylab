The math behind it

new annual salary = current salary × (1 + raise % ÷ 100)
raise per month = (new salary − current salary) ÷ 12
hourly rate = annual salary ÷ (hours per week × weeks per year)
raise needed for a target = (target − current) ÷ current × 100%

Example: $60,000 with a 5% raise becomes $63,000 a year — $3,000 more a year, $250 more a month, and $1.44 more an hour on a 40-hour, 52-week schedule (2,080 hours a year). The hourly step is schedule-sensitive: the same $60,000 at 20 hours a week over 52 weeks is $57.69 an hour (1,040 hours a year), not $28.85, so enter your real schedule rather than assuming 40 × 52. And remember the percentage trap: 5% is $3,000 at $60,000 but $5,000 at $100,000, so judge an offer in dollars as well as percent.

Frequently asked questions

How is my new salary calculated?

Multiply your current salary by one plus the raise percentage. Example: $60,000 with a 5% raise becomes $63,000 a year — $3,000 more a year, $250 more a month, and $1.44 more an hour on a 40-hour, 52-week schedule.

Why does the hourly figure depend on my schedule?

Hourly pay is annual salary divided by hours worked in the year. A $60,000 salary is $28.85 an hour at 40 hours over 52 weeks (2,080 hours), but part-time or seasonal schedules divide by fewer hours, so the same salary is a higher hourly rate.

What raise do I need to reach a target salary?

Divide the gap by your current salary. To go from $60,000 to $66,000 you need a 10% raise ($6,000 ÷ $60,000). Use the reverse lookup above: enter your target salary and it gives the percentage, the annual gap, and the monthly gap.

Is a percentage raise the same as a flat raise?

No. A 5% raise on $60,000 is $3,000, but 5% on $100,000 is $5,000 — the same percentage is worth more at a higher salary. A flat $3,000 raise is 5% at $60,000 but only 3% at $100,000. Compare offers in both dollars and percent before judging them.

Are these figures before or after tax?

Before tax. All figures here are gross: the raise is $3,000 a year gross, and what lands in your account is less after tax and deductions. To see the after-tax side, run the new salary through the take-home pay calculator.

Can I use this for a pay cut?

The math works in reverse — a negative percentage is a cut — but this tool only accepts raises of 0% or more, so a cut is not silently priced as a raise. To price a cut, compute the new salary directly: current salary times one plus the cut percentage (for example, $60,000 × 0.95 = $57,000 for a 5% cut).
