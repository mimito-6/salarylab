The math behind it

working hours = hours per week × weeks per year − PTO days × (hours per week ÷ 5)
total compensation = salary + bonus + benefits − commute cost × 12
effective hourly rate = total compensation ÷ working hours

Example, with the defaults above: Offer A pays $60,000 for 40 hours a week with 10 PTO days — 2,000 working hours, so $30.00 an effective hour (its headline rate is only $28.85, because the headline divides by 2,080 scheduled hours and ignores the paid days off). Offer B pays $70,000 for 45 hours a week with 15 PTO days, a $5,000 bonus, $2,000 of benefits, and a $200 monthly commute — $74,600 total compensation over 2,205 working hours, so $33.83 an effective hour. Offer B wins on both measures here, but that is not guaranteed: an $80,000 offer at 40 hours a week ($38.46 an hour) loses on hourly rate to a $78,000 offer at 35 hours a week ($42.86 an hour), even though it pays $2,000 more a year. When the two verdicts split, the calculator says so — the choice is then time versus money, not a math error.

Frequently asked questions

Why isn't the higher salary always the better offer?

Salary alone ignores how many hours buy it. An $80,000 offer at 40 hours a week pays $38.46 an effective hour, while a $78,000 offer at 35 hours a week pays $42.86 — the lower salary is $4.40 an hour better, even though it pays $2,000 less a year. Add paid time off, bonuses, benefits, and commute cost, and the headline number moves even further.

How are paid days off counted?

Paid time off is paid, so it shrinks your working hours, not your pay. Working hours equal scheduled hours minus PTO days times hours per day (hours per week divided by 5). Example: 40 hours a week over 52 weeks is 2,080 scheduled hours; 10 PTO days remove 80 hours, leaving 2,000 working hours — so a $60,000 salary is $30.00 an effective hour, not $28.85.

What counts as "other benefits"?

Anything the employer pays on top of salary and bonus, priced per year: 401(k) match, stock or RSUs, and health-insurance premiums the employer covers. Enter the annual dollar value. If you cannot price a benefit, leave it at $0 rather than guessing — the comparison stays honest about what it does and does not include.

Why subtract the commute cost?

Commute cost comes out of the same pay, so an offer that costs $200 a month to reach is really $2,400 a year less. The calculator subtracts twelve months of commute cost from salary, bonus, and benefits to give total compensation. For the exact commute figure, use the commute cost calculator.

What if one offer wins on total pay and the other on hourly rate?

That split is real and the calculator shows both verdicts instead of picking one for you. The higher-total offer pays more money; the higher-hourly offer buys that money with fewer of your hours. Choosing between them is a time-versus-money decision, not a math error.

Are these figures before or after tax?

Before tax. All figures here are gross: total compensation and effective hourly rate are pre-tax. To see what each offer pays after deductions, run the winning total through the take-home pay calculator.
