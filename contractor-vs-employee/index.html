The math behind it

employee working hours = weekly hours × (52 − PTO days ÷ 5)
employee total compensation = salary + benefits
employee effective hourly rate = total compensation ÷ working hours
contractor gross = hourly rate × billable hours per week × billable weeks
self-employment tax = gross × 0.9235 × SE rate
contractor net = gross − self-employment tax
contractor effective hourly rate = net ÷ billable hours
break-even contractor rate = employee total compensation ÷ (1 − 0.9235 × SE rate) ÷ billable hours

Worked example: an $80,000 salary plus $12,000 of benefits is $92,000 of total compensation. With 15 paid days off — 3 weeks — a 40-hour week means 40 × 49 = 1,960 working hours, so the employee's effective rate is $92,000 ÷ 1,960 = $46.94 an hour. The contractor at $60 an hour, 40 billable hours a week for 48 weeks, grosses $115,200; self-employment tax is $115,200 × 0.9235 × 15.3% = $16,277.24, leaving $98,922.76 net, or $51.52 an hour over 1,920 billable hours. Here the contractor wins both ways — $51.52 vs $46.94 an hour, and $98,922.76 vs $92,000 for the year. The break-even rate is $92,000 ÷ 0.8587 ÷ 1,920 = $55.80 an hour: below that, the employee package pays more; above it, contracting does.

The two winners can split. Take the same contractor at 30 billable hours a week for 40 weeks: the gross falls to $72,000 and the net to $61,826.72, but the effective rate is still $51.52 an hour — the rate after self-employment tax does not depend on hours. So the contractor still wins per hour ($51.52 vs $46.94) while the employee wins in total ($92,000 vs $61,826.72), and the break-even rate jumps to $89.28 an hour. That split is the honest answer: a higher rate is not a higher paycheck unless the hours are there too.

Frequently asked questions

Why compare effective hourly rates instead of just the salaries?

Because the headline numbers are not measured in the same units. A $80,000 salary with $12,000 of benefits and 15 paid days off is $92,000 for 1,960 working hours — $46.94 an hour. A $60-an-hour contractor rate is $115,200 gross for 1,920 billable hours, but $16,277.24 of that is self-employment tax, leaving $98,922.76, or $51.52 an hour. Comparing $80,000 to $60 an hour directly tells you nothing; the effective hourly rates do.

Are these figures before or after income tax?

Before income tax. This page compares gross compensation only: employee salary plus benefits against contractor gross minus self-employment tax. Income tax is not modelled, because the two sides pay it differently — the employee has tax withheld, the contractor pays estimated tax and can deduct business expenses. To see what a salary pays after deductions, use the take-home pay calculator.

What is the 92.35% in the self-employment tax?

A US rule: only 92.35% of a contractor's net earnings are subject to self-employment tax. The tax itself is 15.3% — 12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare — so the effective rate on gross is 0.9235 × 15.3% = 14.13%. On $115,200 of gross that is $16,277.24. This is an approximation of the US rule, not tax advice, and the rate is editable if your situation differs.

What should I put in for benefits value?

Whatever the employer pays on your behalf, in dollars per year: health insurance premiums, retirement match, life insurance, and similar. Ask HR for the total if you can. If you do not know the value, enter $0 — then read the result as a salary-only comparison, and remember the employee side is understated by exactly the benefits you left out.

Why can the per-hour winner and the total winner be different?

Because hours differ. A contractor at $60 an hour who bills 30 hours a week for 40 weeks still earns $51.52 an hour after self-employment tax — more than the employee's $46.94 — but only $61,826.72 for the year, well under the employee's $92,000. The per-hour winner is the better rate; the total winner is the bigger paycheck. When they split, it is a time-vs-money choice, not a clear winner.

What doesn't this calculator capture?

Three things the numbers cannot see. First, the employee gets paid sick leave, unemployment protection, and an employer safety net; the contractor gets none of that. Second, unpaid non-billable time — admin, finding clients, invoicing — is not in billable hours, so if you spend 10 hours a week on it, enter fewer billable hours. Third, contractor income varies: billable hours are a plan, not a guarantee, while the employee salary is fixed.
