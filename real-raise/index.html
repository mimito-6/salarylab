The math behind it

new nominal salary = current salary × (1 + raise % ÷ 100)
salary that keeps pace = current salary × (1 + inflation % ÷ 100)
new salary in today's dollars = new nominal salary ÷ (1 + inflation % ÷ 100)
real raise % = (1 + raise % ÷ 100) ÷ (1 + inflation % ÷ 100) − 1
nominal raise for a target real raise = (1 + real target % ÷ 100) × (1 + inflation % ÷ 100) − 1

Example: $60,000 with a 5% raise and 3% inflation becomes $63,000 nominal — but $63,000 ÷ 1.03 = $61,165.05 in today's dollars, a real raise of 1.94%, or $1,165.05 a year of extra buying power. Inflation ate $1,800 of the $3,000 raise (the salary needed just to keep pace is $61,800), leaving a $1,200 nominal surplus. The subtraction shortcut — 5% minus 3% is 2% — is close here but wrong: the exact figure is 1.94%, because the raise and the inflation apply to different bases. The gap widens when the numbers get bigger: a 20% raise against 15% inflation is 4.35% real, not 5%.

And a raise below inflation is a pay cut in disguise: $80,000 with a 2% raise at 3.5% inflation becomes $81,600, but only $78,840.58 in today's dollars — a real cut of 1.45%, or $1,159.42 a year of buying power gone. A 0% raise at 4% inflation is worse still: $50,000 becomes $48,076.92 in today's dollars, a 3.85% real cut. The break-even line is simple: your nominal raise must equal inflation to stand still.

Frequently asked questions

Why isn't the real raise just the raise minus inflation?

Because the two percentages apply to different bases. The exact real raise is (1 + raise) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. A 5% raise with 3% inflation is 1.05 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 1.94%, not 2%. Subtracting is a close approximation only when both numbers are small — at 20% raise and 15% inflation it gives 5% while the true figure is 4.35%.

What does "in today's dollars" mean?

It is the new salary restated at today's prices: new salary divided by one plus inflation. A $63,000 salary after 3% inflation is worth $61,165.05 in today's dollars — it buys what $61,165.05 buys today. The $1,834.95 gap is the part inflation ate.

My raise is below inflation — did I get a pay cut?

In purchasing power, yes. A 2% raise with 3.5% inflation on $80,000 takes you to $81,600, but that is only $78,840.58 in today's dollars — a real pay cut of 1.45%, or $1,159.42 a year of buying power. The number on the paycheck went up; what it buys went down.

What raise do I need just to keep pace with inflation?

Exactly the inflation rate. At 3% inflation, a 3% raise is break-even: $60,000 becomes $61,800, which buys exactly what $60,000 bought before. Anything below 3% is a real pay cut; anything above is a real raise.

Are these figures before or after tax?

Before tax. All figures here are gross. Inflation shrinks purchasing power before tax even enters the picture; to see what the new salary pays after deductions, run it through the take-home pay calculator.

Can I use this for a raise over several years?

This page prices one raise against one year's inflation. For a salary compared across several years, use the inflation-adjusted salary calculator, which compounds inflation year by year.
