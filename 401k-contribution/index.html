The math behind it

your contribution = salary × contribution % (capped at the 2026 IRS limit of $24,500)
employer match = min(your contribution, salary × match cap %) × match %
match left on the table = max(0, salary × match cap % − your contribution) × match %
future value = annual total × ((1 + return)^years − 1) ÷ return

Example: $80,000 salary, contributing 6%, with a 50% match up to 6%. Your contribution is $4,800 a year ($400 a month); the employer adds $2,400, so $7,200 goes in every year — 9.00% of your salary, $600 a month. Over 30 years at 7%, that annual $7,200 grows to $680,117.66: $453,411.77 from your own contributions and $226,705.89 from the match. The match alone is a third of the final balance — money you would never see if you contributed nothing.

Contribute less and the loss is exact, not vague. At the same $100,000 salary with a 100% match up to 4%, contributing 3% puts in $3,000 and earns a $3,000 match — but contributing 4% would earn $4,000. The missing 1% costs $1,000 of free money every year, and over 30 years at 7% that $1,000-a-year gap compounds to about $94,461. The return assumption matters too: the same $7,200 a year at 5% instead of 7% reaches only $478,359.70 after 30 years — $201,758 less. The projection is deliberately conservative: it assumes each year's contribution lands at year end, so real monthly paycheck contributions would grow a little more.

One more ceiling worth knowing: a 10% contribution on a $300,000 salary would be $30,000, but the 2026 IRS limit caps your own contribution at $24,500 — the match is then calculated on the capped amount ($9,000 at 50% up to 6%), for a $33,500 total. The $24,500 limit does not include the match; the combined $72,000 ceiling from all sources is never reached by a match alone, because the match is at most your own capped contribution.

Frequently asked questions

What does "50% match up to 6%" actually mean?

Your employer adds 50 cents for every dollar you contribute, but only on the first 6% of your salary. On an $80,000 salary, contributing 6% ($4,800) earns the full $2,400 match. Contributing 10% ($6,000) still earns only $2,400 — the match stops at 6% of salary. Contributing 3% ($2,400) earns only $1,200, leaving $1,200 of free match on the table.

Does the employer match count toward my $24,500 limit?

No. The 2026 IRS limit of $24,500 caps only what you put in from your own paycheck. The employer match sits on top of it. The combined ceiling from all sources is $72,000 a year, but a match alone can never push you over it — the match is at most your own (capped) contribution, so the combined total tops out at $49,000 in this calculator.

What if my contribution rate would put in more than $24,500?

The calculator caps your contribution at $24,500 and says so. A 10% rate on a $300,000 salary would be $30,000, so only $24,500 goes in and the match is calculated on the capped amount. If you are 50 or older, the catch-up lets you add $8,000 more ($32,500 total); at ages 60–63 the higher catch-up is $11,250 ($35,750 total). This tool uses the under-50 limit.

Is the employer match really free money?

Yes — a 50% match is an instant 50% return on the matched dollars, before any investment growth. The one catch is vesting: many plans only let you keep the full match after a few years with the company, so if you leave early you may forfeit part of it. Your own contributions are always 100% yours. Check your plan's vesting schedule.

Why does the projection assume end-of-year contributions?

It is a deliberate, conservative simplification: each year's contribution is assumed to land at year end, so it earns no return in the year it is made. Real paycheck contributions go in every pay period and earn a little more — monthly deposits over 30 years at 7% would add roughly 7–8% to the total. The projection also assumes a flat return every year; real markets vary.

Should I contribute enough to get the full match before anything else?

For most people, yes — the match is the highest guaranteed return available anywhere, and turning it down is leaving part of your pay on the table. This tool only does the math; it is not financial advice. High-interest debt and an emergency fund are the usual competing priorities, and your plan administrator can confirm your exact match formula.
