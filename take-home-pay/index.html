The math behind it

tax per paycheck = gross × (tax rate ÷ 100)
take-home per paycheck = gross − tax − other deductions
take-home per year = take-home per paycheck × paychecks per year
take-home per month = take-home per year ÷ 12

Example: $2,375 gross biweekly at 22% gives $522.50 of tax; minus $150 of other deductions, take-home pay is $1,702.50 per paycheck — $44,265 a year, $3,688.75 a month, and 71.7% of gross. Note the monthly step: it divides the annual figure by 12, it does not multiply a biweekly check by 2 (there are 26 biweekly checks a year, not 24). The flat rate is an approximation: real income tax is progressive, so the closest input is the effective rate from your own pay stub, and this tool is arithmetic, not tax advice.

Frequently asked questions

How is my take-home pay calculated?

Tax withheld is your gross pay times your tax rate; subtract that and your other deductions from gross pay. Example: $2,375 gross biweekly at a 22% tax rate gives $522.50 of tax; minus $150 of other deductions, take-home pay is $1,702.50 per paycheck, or $44,265 a year over 26 paychecks.

Why is a single flat tax rate only an approximation?

Real income tax is progressive: different slices of income are taxed at different rates, and credits, deductions, and state rules change the total. A flat rate is a useful estimate, not a tax return. For the closest estimate, use the effective rate from your own pay stub (total tax withheld divided by gross pay) rather than your top marginal bracket.

Where do I find my tax rate?

Look at a recent pay stub: divide the total tax withheld on that check (federal plus state, if any) by the gross pay on the same check. If $522.50 of tax was withheld from $2,375 gross, your effective rate is 22%. Using that figure already reflects your pre-tax deductions approximately, because it comes from your real withholding.

What counts as other deductions?

Anything taken out of your check that is not tax: health insurance premiums, retirement contributions, union dues, and similar items. Enter the total per paycheck. If an item is pre-tax, your pay-stub effective rate already reflects it, so do not also try to adjust the tax rate for it — just include the dollar amount here.

Why is my monthly take-home not twice my biweekly take-home?

Because biweekly pay has 26 paychecks a year and a month averages 26 ÷ 12 = 2.1667 paychecks, not 2. A $1,702.50 biweekly check is $3,688.75 a month ($44,265 ÷ 12), not $3,405. The same applies in reverse: a monthly check is simply its own amount, since there are 12 of them.

Is this tax advice?

No. This tool only does the arithmetic on the rate and deductions you enter. It does not estimate your tax liability, choose a withholding amount, or replace a tax professional. For decisions about withholding, use your employer's withholding form and, if unsure, a tax professional.
