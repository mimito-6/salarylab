The math behind it

price factor = (1 + inflation rate) ^ years
equivalent salary = salary × price factor
difference = equivalent salary − salary
cumulative price change (%) = (price factor − 1) × 100
what your salary buys then = salary ÷ price factor

Example: $50,000 ten years ago at 3% average inflation. The price factor is (1.03)^10 = 1.3439, so the equivalent salary today is $50,000 × 1.3439 = $67,195.82 — $17,195.82 more, or +34.39%, which is $5,599.65 a month against $4,166.67 then. And $50,000 today buys what $37,204.70 bought ten years ago ($50,000 ÷ 1.3439). Looking forward works the same way: $80,000 today, five years at 2.5%, needs $90,512.66 to keep the same purchasing power. Over longer horizons the compounding bites harder — $60,000 twenty years ago at 3% needs $108,366.67 today — which is why the rule of 72 is a handy check: at 3% inflation, prices roughly double every 24 years (72 ÷ 3), and (1.03)^24 = 2.03 confirms it.

Frequently asked questions

What inflation rate should I use?

There is no single right number — it depends on the country and the years. In the United States, consumer prices have averaged roughly 2–3% a year over recent decades, and the Federal Reserve targets 2%. Use your country's actual average for the period you care about, and run a second scenario at a higher rate to see how sensitive the answer is: at 3%, a $50,000 salary needs $67,195.82 after ten years; at 5%, it needs $81,444.73.

Why compound instead of just multiplying the rate by the years?

Because each year's price increase applies to prices that already rose the year before. Simple multiplication says 3% a year for ten years is 30%, or $50,000 × 1.30 = $65,000. Compounding gives (1.03)^10 = 1.3439, or $67,195.82 — the $2,195.82 gap is the interest on the interest, and it grows with every extra year.

Does this include pay raises?

No. This calculator prices the same purchasing power, not your career. If your salary grew faster than inflation over the period, you gained real pay; if it grew slower, you lost real pay even though the number went up. Compare your actual salary path against the equivalent salary to see the real change.

What about deflation, when prices fall?

The same formula works with a negative rate: a $50,000 salary ten years ago at −1% a year needs only $45,219.10 today to buy the same basket, because prices fell about 9.6% over the decade. This calculator accepts rates down to −5%.

Is the equivalent salary what I should ask for in a negotiation?

It is the floor, not the target. The equivalent salary leaves you exactly where you were — anything above it is a real raise, anything below it is a real pay cut even if the headline number went up. A raise that only matches inflation is a cost-of-living adjustment, not a promotion.

How is this different from the cost of living salary calculator?

That tool translates a salary across cities at the same moment — same job, same life, different prices. This one translates a salary across time in the same city — same job, same life, later prices. Use this one to check whether your pay kept up with inflation, and the cost of living calculator to check what the same pay is worth somewhere else.
