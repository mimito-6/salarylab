The math behind it

equivalent salary = current salary × (new city index ÷ current city index)
difference = equivalent salary − current salary
cost difference (%) = (new city index ÷ current city index − 1) × 100
what your salary buys there = current salary ÷ (new city index ÷ current city index)

Example: $80,000 a year in a city indexed at 100, moving to a city indexed at 120. The equivalent salary is $80,000 × 120 ÷ 100 = $96,000 — $16,000 more, or +20%, which is $8,000 a month against $6,666.67 now. And if you keep the $80,000, it buys in the new city what $66,666.67 buys at home ($80,000 ÷ 1.2). The other direction works the same way: $60,000 in a city indexed at 85, moving to one indexed at 70, needs only $49,411.76 — $10,588.24 less, or −17.65% — and the same $60,000 there buys what $72,857.14 buys at home. When the two indices are equal, the equivalent salary is your current salary and the difference is zero: the ratio is the whole calculation, so the absolute scale of the index never matters, only that both numbers come from the same source on the same base.

Frequently asked questions

Where do I find cost of living index numbers?

From a cost of living index publisher such as Numbeo, NerdWallet, or ERI. Numbeo's Cost of Living Index sets New York City at 100, so a city at 120 is about 20% more expensive than New York and a city at 80 is about 20% cheaper. The one rule that matters: take both cities' numbers from the same source, on the same base. Mixing sources mixes methods, and the ratio — which is all this calculator uses — stops meaning anything.

What does an index of 120 mean?

That the average basket of goods and services — groceries, rent, transport, restaurants, and the rest — costs about 20% more than in the index-100 baseline city. An $80,000 salary in the baseline city needs $96,000 in the 120 city to buy the same basket: $80,000 × 120 ÷ 100 = $96,000.

My equivalent salary is higher — does that mean I am richer?

No. The equivalent salary buys exactly the same life, not a better one: the extra $16,000 in the example above is eaten entirely by higher prices. If the new job pays more than the equivalent salary, that surplus is the real raise; if it pays less, you are taking a pay cut in purchasing power even though the headline number went up.

Does this include taxes?

No. Everything here is gross salary, and the index itself says nothing about state or local tax — a city can be cheap to live in and expensive to be taxed in, or the reverse. Treat the equivalent salary as a gross figure, then run it through the take-home pay calculator if you want the after-tax picture in each place.

What if housing is most of my spending?

Then the headline index will understate the move for you, because the index averages everything and housing is the most variable part — it can be half your budget in one city and a quarter in another. Compare the rent for the kind of place you would actually live in separately, and sanity-check it against the calculator's answer before you sign anything.
